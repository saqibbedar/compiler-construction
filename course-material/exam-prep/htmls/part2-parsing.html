Chapter 1Lecture 13 · 20 Oct

Why regular expressions give up

After lexical analysis we have a SERIES OF TOKENS.

LIMIT OF REGULAR EXPRESSION

    can check PATTERNS
    but not VALIDATE SYNTAX

That one line from your notes carries the whole rest of the course. Here is why it is true.

The game

You are given a machine. It reads characters one at a time, left to right. It has a fixed, finite number of states — say 50 little boxes it can sit in. It has no notepad. After each character it just moves to another box.

That machine is a finite automaton, and it is exactly what a regular expression compiles into.

Your task: use it to check that brackets are balanced. Accept (()). Reject (().

Why you will fail — and it is not your fault

To know whether brackets balance you must remember how many are still open. With 50 boxes you can count to about 50. Feed it 51 open brackets and it loses track — it has physically run out of boxes to remember with.

But a program may nest 51 deep. Or 500. There is no upper limit. A machine with finite memory and no notepad cannot count without a bound.

And a programming language is nothing but nesting: brackets inside brackets, if inside while inside a function. Nesting is not a corner case — it is the subject.

Said another way

CAN the scanner answer:   "is 'while20' a legal identifier?"
                           YES — it is just a pattern.  ✓

CAN the scanner answer:   "is '( int + ) * int' a legal expression?"
                           NO.  ✗

   every single token in it is legal.
   it is the ARRANGEMENT that is broken —
   and arrangement is not a pattern.

Chapter 2Lecture 13 · 20 Oct

Context-free grammars

Definition — context-free grammar

A CFG is four things, G = (N, T, S, P):

T   TERMINALS      the real tokens that survive into the
                   finished program: int, +, (, while
                   they TERMINATE — nothing replaces them

N   NON-TERMINALS  names for structures, written as capitals:
                   E, T, P, Op
                   every one MUST eventually be replaced

S   START SYMBOL   the one non-terminal you begin from

P   PRODUCTIONS    the rules, each written  A → something
Why "context-free"?

Because the left side of every rule is a single non-terminal. If a rule says Op → +, you may apply it anywhere an Op appears — it never depends on what sits around it. The surroundings (the "context") are irrelevant.

The notation, once

SymbolSay it as
→ or ::="can be replaced by"
|"or" — shorthand for several rules with the same left side
ε (epsilon)"nothing at all" — the empty string
⇒"becomes, in one step" — used between lines of working, not inside a rule

The first grammar from your lecture

E  → int
E  → E Op E
E  → ( E )
Op → + | − | *
T = { int , + , − , * , ( , ) }
N = { E , Op }
S = E
Spot the superpower

Look at E → ( E ). The E appears on both sides. That is recursion, and it is the notepad the finite automaton was missing: the rule can apply to itself forever, so brackets nest to any depth from one line of grammar.

Exactly what chapter 1 said was impossible for regular expressions.

The warning your notes attach to it

E → int , E → E Op E , E → ( E )
Op → + , Op → − , Op → *

         ✗  THIS DOES NOT HANDLE PRECEDENCE

Chapter 5 shows exactly what goes wrong. Chapter 6 fixes it.

A note on notation you will see in the slides

ConventionMeans
A, B, Cnon-terminals
t, u, v, wterminals
α, β, γ, ωarbitrary strings of terminals and non-terminals
α ⇒ ββ derives from α in one step
α ⇒* ββ derives from α in zero or more steps
A CFG is not shorthand notation

Inside a CFG you may not use *, | or brackets as regular-expression operators. (The | for "or" between whole alternatives is fine; a* meaning "repeat" is not.) Convert it instead:

WANT:   S → a* b

WRITE:  S → A b
        A → A a | ε

A likely exam question. The rule: repetition becomes a recursive non-terminal with an ε alternative.

Derivations, leftmost and rightmost

Definition — derivation

A derivation is the list of replacement steps from the start symbol down to a string of pure terminals.

Each half-finished line — one that still contains non-terminals — is a sentential form [a snapshot of the work in progress].

The derivation from your notes

For int + int * int, using E → int | E Op E. The right-hand column says what changed — your notes leave that for you to work out.

  (i)    E
  (ii)   E Op E                  E → E Op E
  (iii)  E Op E Op E             the right E, same rule
  (iv)   E Op E * E              Op → *
  (v)    int Op E * E            E → int
  (vi)   int Op int * E          E → int
  (vii)  int Op int * int        E → int
  (viii) int + int * int         Op → +     ✓ finished

Nothing clever happens. Pick a non-terminal, look up a rule, swap it out. When none remain, the grammar accepts the string.

What a derivation proves

If you can derive a string, it is grammatically legal. If no derivation exists, it is a syntax error. That is the parser's whole job restated: find a derivation, or prove there isn't one.

Leftmost and rightmost

At line (iii) above there were two E's waiting. Which first? Fixing that choice gives the two names your lecture lists.

Definition
  • Leftmost derivation — always replace the leftmost non-terminal.
  • Rightmost derivation — always replace the rightmost one. Also called the canonical derivation.

Top-down parsers naturally produce leftmost derivations; bottom-up parsers produce rightmost ones, in reverse.

Leftmost
E
⇒ E + E
⇒ int + E
⇒ int + E * E
⇒ int + int * E
⇒ int + int * int
Rightmost
E
⇒ E + E
⇒ E + E * E
⇒ E + E * int
⇒ E + int * int
⇒ int + int * int

The pointDifferent order of working, same final string, same structure. The order you happen to work in is not important — what matters is which rules you chose. That is chapter 5.

Parse trees and the AST

Definition — parse tree

A parse tree is a derivation drawn as a tree:

  • the root is the start symbol;
  • every internal node is a non-terminal, and its children are exactly the right-hand side of the rule used on it;
  • every leaf is a terminal.

Read the leaves left to right and your original program comes back. Your notes call this the left-order traversal.

int + int * int

          E
       ╱  │  ╲
      E   +   E
      │     ╱ │ ╲
     int   E  *  E
           │     │
          int   int

leaves, left to right:  int + int * int   ✓

The example from the slides

The statement while (ip < z) ++ip; — simplified to operators and operands:

            while
            ╱    ╲
          ╱        ╲
        <            ++
      ╱   ╲           │
    ip     z         ip

read as:  a WHILE, whose condition is (ip < z),
          and whose body is (++ip)

Parse tree vs abstract syntax tree

Definition — AST

An abstract syntax tree is the parse tree with the scaffolding removed: no helper non-terminals, no brackets, just operators and operands.

The parse tree records how the grammar was applied. The AST records what the program means.

Parse tree for a*b+c
        ⟨expr⟩
      ╱   │   ╲
  ⟨expr⟩  +  ⟨term⟩
     │          │
  ⟨term⟩   ⟨primary⟩
  ╱  │  ╲       │
⟨term⟩ * ⟨prim⟩ c
  │        │
⟨prim⟩     b
  │
  a
AST for a*b+c
      ( + )
      ╱   ╲
   ( * )    c
   ╱   ╲
  a     b
and for  x = 2 + 3 :

        ( = )
        ╱   ╲
  ⟨id: x⟩   ( + )
            ╱   ╲
     ⟨num: 2⟩   ⟨num: 3⟩
Why the tree, not the list, is the real answer

A derivation records the order you happened to work in — an accident of how you were writing. A tree records the structure: which operator owns which operands.

The next phase generates code by walking this tree. So the tree is, literally, the meaning of the program. Which leads straight to the catastrophe in chapter 5: if one program yields two different trees, it has two different meanings.

Semantic actions — a preview

Definition — semantic action

A semantic action is a small piece of code attached to a grammar rule, which runs when that rule is applied. Building output while parsing, rather than afterwards, is called syntax-directed translation.

GRAMMAR RULE              SEMANTIC ACTION

E1 → E2 + T               E1.val = E2.val + T.val
E  → T                    E.val  = T.val
T  → int                  T.val  = int.lexval

parsing  2 + 3  now also COMPUTES 5,
with no separate pass.

The subscriptsE1 and E2 are the same non-terminal E, numbered only so the action can say which one it means — the parent or the child.

Same grammar. Same input. Two trees. Two different answers.

Definition — ambiguous grammar

A grammar is ambiguous if there exists at least one string with two or more different parse trees.

Your notes, word for word: "any grammar having 2 parse trees is called ambiguous grammar." Equivalently: two different leftmost derivations.

Where the ambiguity comes from — the question from your notes

Your page asks it directly: "How do we decide that + opens first or *?" And answers it: "op: + | − | * | / → yeh sab aik hi level pay hain" — they are all at the same level.

That is the disease, named. The rule Op → + | − | * | / treats all four operators as interchangeable. The grammar has no way to say that * is stronger than +. Nothing in the rules prefers one tree.

The consequence

Your notes: "Impossible to write a top-down parser for an ambiguous grammar."

Why: a top-down parser must choose a rule from the token it can see. Looking at 3, both E → E + E and E → E * E are possible, and nothing distinguishes them. The parser either guesses and later undoes its work, or it stalls.

So you fix the grammar before writing any parser.

The second flavour

E → int | E − E on 8 − 3 − 2:

  (8 − 3) − 2 = 3        vs        8 − (3 − 2) = 7

Both operators are identical here, so "which is stronger" cannot help. What is missing is a rule about which side groups first.

Chapter 6Lecture 13 · 20 Oct

Fixing precedence by layering

First the intuition. Why does * "bind tighter" than +?

Picture 3 + 4 * 5 as three people and two ropes. The * rope is short and tight: it pulls 4 and 5 into a single lump. The + rope is long and loose: it then ties 3 to that whole lump. The tight rope is tied first, so it ends up deeper inside.

The visual ruleTighter-binding operators sit lower in the parse tree. Looser ones sit higher, near the root.

Your notes name the technique: "Resolving ambiguity — layering — introduce new non-terminal."

The layering rule

Give each precedence level its own non-terminal. Loosest at the top, tightest at the bottom. Each layer may only reach downward to the layer beneath — never sideways, never up.

Why that one-way street fixes everything

If the + layer can only be built out of *-layer pieces, then a * can never end up above a +. The wrong tree is not rejected — it is impossible to construct. Nothing is left to be ambiguous about.

Building it, layer by layer

LayerNameOperatorsBinds
1 — topE — Expression+ −loosest
2T — Term* /tighter
3P — Power^tighter still
4 — bottomF — Factorint, ( E )tightest: the atoms
E → E + T  |  E − T  |  T
T → T * P  |  T / P  |  P
P → F ^ P  |  F
F → int    |  ( E )

Read line 1 aloud"An expression is: an expression, plus a term — or an expression, minus a term — or just a term on its own."

Every line has the same shape: either apply my own operator and then drop one layer, or pass straight through to the layer below.

The derivation from your lecture, annotated

Your notes derive int + int * int in this grammar. Watch the layers do the work. (Rightmost derivation — it replaces the rightmost non-terminal each time.)

  (i)    E
  (ii)   E + T              only E → E + T puts a + at the top
  (iii)  E + T * P          the T becomes T * P — the * lands BELOW the +
  (iv)   T + T * P          left E drops a layer:  E → T
  (v)    T + T * int        P → F → int
  (vi)   P + T * int        T → P
  (vii)  P + P * int        T → P again
  (viii) int + int * int    P → F → int, twice      ✓
The crucial moment is line (iii)

After line (ii) the right-hand side is a T, not an E. And a T has no rule containing + — check the grammar. So that side physically cannot produce another +; it can only produce * and below.

Not a preference. A structural impossibility. Unambiguous.

the only tree this grammar can build for 3 + 4 * 5:

Then what are brackets for?

The escape hatch

F → ( E ) is the way out. You have fallen to the bottom layer, and this rule throws you straight back to the top. That is exactly why (3+4)*5 works: brackets let a loose operator appear beneath a tight one, which layering would otherwise forbid.

And this does NOT reintroduce ambiguity 
3 + 4 * 5         one input
( 3 + 4 ) * 5     a DIFFERENT input

Those are not the same program — the brackets are real tokens in the file. Two different strings are allowed to have two different trees; that is correctness, not ambiguity. Ambiguity means one string with two trees.

layering  →  what happens when the programmer says nothing
brackets  →  the programmer overriding that default, out loud

Associativity

Layering sorted out different operators. Now: the same operator, twice in a row.

ExpressionMeansGroups from
3 + 4 + 5(3+4)+5the left
3 × 4 × 5(3×4)×5the left
3 ^ 4 ^ 53^(4^5)the right ← the odd one out
Why power is the odd one out

(3^4)^5 is already written more simply as 3^20 — the left-grouping reading would make the double-power notation pointless. Mathematics therefore reads towers of powers from the top down. Your notes say it plainly: "Power is a right associative operator."

For + and − the reason is different: 8 − 3 − 2 must be 3, not 7, so they group from the left.

The rule that controls it

Memorise these two shapes 
A → A op B | B      recursion on the LEFT
                    ⇒ LEFT-associative
                    ⇒ (a op b) op c

A → B op A | B      recursion on the RIGHT
                    ⇒ RIGHT-associative
                    ⇒ a op (b op c)

The tree grows on the side the recursion is on.

Your notes state it in the lecturer's words: "power is right associative, so wo wala symbol power kay baad aaye ga" — the repeating symbol goes after the ^.

8.1 Balanced parentheses

This is the example that proved regular expressions were not enough (chapter 1), so it is satisfying to see a CFG do it in two lines.

P → P S | ε
S → ( P )

Read itA paren-sequence is a paren-sequence followed by one more balanced group, or nothing at all. A group is an open bracket, a paren-sequence, a close bracket.

trace  ( ( ) ( ) )

P ⇒ P S ⇒ ε S ⇒ S
  ⇒ ( P )
  ⇒ ( P S )

  ⇒ ( S S )
  ⇒ ( ( P ) S )   ⇒ ( ( ) S )

Why imbalance is impossible

( ( ) simply cannot be derived — every ( introduced by S → ( P ) arrives with its matching ) attached. The grammar makes imbalance unconstructible, which is stronger than checking for it afterwards.

Check this against your slides

Your notes write the two rules as P → P S | ε and P → ( S ). The second must use a different non-terminal on the left — S → ( P ) — or the two layers collapse into one and the grammar stops working.

8.2 A grammar for regular expressions

A nice twist from your notes: writing a CFG whose language is the set of regular expressions. The first attempt is ambiguous.

R → a | b | c | …          AMBIGUOUS
R → ε
R → R R                    concatenation
R → R "|" R                alternation
R → R *                    repetition
R → ( R )

Ambiguous for exactly the reason of chapter 5: a|b c could group as a|(bc) or (a|b)c. The cure is the same — layer it, loosest operator at the top. Precedence of regex operators: | loosest, then concatenation, then * tightest.

R → S | R "|" S            layer 1:  alternation   (loosest)
S → T | S T                layer 2:  concatenation
T → U | T *                layer 3:  star          (tightest)
U → a | b | c | …          layer 4:  atoms
U → "ε"
U → ( R )

Compare that shape with the E / T / P / F grammar in chapter 6. It is the identical pattern: four layers, each reaching only downward, atoms and brackets at the bottom. Learn the pattern once and you can disambiguate any operator grammar they put in front of you.

Drill — in the layered regex grammar, how does a|bc* group?

a | (b (c*)). The * is in the lowest layer so it binds to c alone; concatenation is the middle layer so it joins b and c*; | is the top layer so it splits the whole thing last.

8.3 A grammar for statements

From the slides, the shape a real language takes:

STMT  → if EXPR then STMT else STMT
      | while EXPR do STMT
      | EXPR

EXPR  → EXPR + EXPR
      | EXPR − EXPR
      | id

BLOCK → { STMTS }
STMTS → STMT ; STMTS | ε

Every construct you write daily is one of these rules. The if … else line in particular is the source of chapter 21's problem.

Chapter 9Slides

Facts about ambiguity

Three statements that are easy to get wrong in an exam, and one question worth asking.

Fact 1 — ambiguity belongs to the GRAMMAR, not the language 
E → int | E + E           AMBIGUOUS
E → E + T | T , T → int   UNAMBIGUOUS

SAME language.  Different grammars.

So "is this language ambiguous?" is usually the wrong question. Ask "is this grammar ambiguous?"

Fact 2 — some languages are INHERENTLY ambiguous

For a few languages, no unambiguous grammar exists at all. Not "hard to find" — mathematically impossible. Rare, and not a programming language you will meet, but the term is examinable.

Fact 3 — there is NO ALGORITHM to detect or remove ambiguity

You cannot write a program that reads any grammar and answers "is this ambiguous?". The problem is undecidable.

Which means disambiguating a grammar is hand work, guided by the patterns in chapters 6 and 7. That is why those patterns are worth memorising — there is no tool to fall back on.

Is ambiguity always a problem?

E → int | E + E

   3 + 4 + 5
   tree 1:  (3+4)+5 = 12
   tree 2:  3+(4+5) = 12      SAME ANSWER

   addition is associative, so it does not matter.


E → int | E + E | E − E

   8 − 3 − 2
   tree 1:  (8−3)−2 = 3
   tree 2:  8−(3−2) = 7       DIFFERENT

   now it matters.

The honest answerIt depends on the semantics. But a compiler must be predictable for every program, not just the lucky ones — so grammars are disambiguated regardless.

Resolving ambiguity outside the grammar

many parser generators (yacc, bison) let you write
PRECEDENCE DECLARATIONS instead of layering:

   %left   '+' '−'
   %left   '*' '/'
   %right  '^'

keep the short ambiguous grammar,
and declare how to break the ties.

Same effect as layering, far less typing. The grammar stays ambiguous; the generator is simply told which tree to prefer. Chapter 21 uses this idea for the dangling else.

Chapter 10Lecture 13 · 20 Oct

Top-down and bottom-up

You have a clean grammar and a list of tokens. You want the tree. There are exactly two directions.

Definition — the two families
  • Top-down parsing — begin at the start symbol and work downward toward the tokens. Builds the tree root first. Produces a leftmost derivation. Example: recursive descent, LL parsers.
  • Bottom-up parsing — begin at the tokens and combine upward until you reach the start symbol. Builds the tree leaves first. Example: LR, SLR, LALR — what yacc generates.
  TOP-DOWN                     BOTTOM-UP
  "I'm building an             "I see a 3 and a 4...
   expression. What's           those make a term...
   it made of?"                 that makes an expression."

       E     ①                       E      ⑤
    ╱  │  ╲                       ╱  │  ╲
   E   +   T   ②                 E   +   T    ④
   │       │                     │       │
   T       P   ③                 T       P    ③
   │       │                     │       │
   3       4   ④                 3       4    ①②

"If backtracking → not a good parser"

Definition — backtracking

Backtracking is when a parser picks a rule, runs forward with it, discovers it was wrong, undoes everything, and tries another.

Why it is unacceptable
  • Speed. In the worst case the work doubles with every wrong turn — exponential time.
  • Error messages. By the time you finally fail, you have thrown away all the places you had been. You can only say "something is wrong somewhere", which helps nobody.

A good parser is predictive: it decides correctly using only the token in front of it, and never takes anything back. That requirement is what forces the grammar surgery in chapters 13–19.

Tree vs graph

The margin note on your page — "diff b/w graph and tree is that only one path to reach any node" — is worth keeping, because the parser depends on it.

in a TREE   each node has exactly ONE parent
            ⇒ exactly ONE route from root to any node
            ⇒ the parse tree is a UNIQUE record of structure

which is why a string with TWO possible trees
is a catastrophe rather than a curiosity.

Recursive descent

The most likely exam question: given a grammar, write the parser. The good news is that it is almost mechanical — the code ends up looking like the grammar.

Definition — recursive descent parsing

A recursive descent parser is a top-down parser written as a set of ordinary functions that call each other. No tables, no generator tools — you write it by hand.

The two rules — the entire method

Rule 1 — every NON-TERMINAL becomes a FUNCTION

Grammar has E, T, P, F? Your parser has E(), T(), P(), F(). A non-terminal on the right-hand side becomes a call to its function.

Rule 2 — every TERMINAL becomes an IF-CHECK

Your notes: "Every terminal → if condition check (but required — then error, not available)."

So: is the current token this terminal? If yes, move on. If no, call error().

the three pieces of shared machinery:

token          // the token we are looking at right now
nextToken()    // ask the scanner for the following one
error(n)       // report error number n

How token and nextToken() move

  input:  ( ( x ) )            // the token list from the scanner
            ▲
            └── a hidden pointer, owned by nextToken()

  token                        // ONE variable holding ONE token
func nextToken() {
    move the pointer forward by one    // step right in the list
    return the token it now points at  // hand that token back
}

so this line:

    token = nextToken();

means: "throw away the token I was holding,
        put the NEXT one in my hand"
  START                  token = (
  input:  ( ( x ) )
          ▲

  token = nextToken()    token = (      // the 2nd bracket now
  input:  ( ( x ) )
            ▲

  token = nextToken()    token = x
  input:  ( ( x ) )
              ▲
The two words 
PEEK / LOOKAHEAD   reading `token` without calling nextToken()
                   free, repeatable

CONSUME / EAT      token = nextToken();
                   happens ONCE, no going back
the bug this prevents:

Example 2 — choosing between alternatives

func A() {

        token = nextToken();
        X();

        Y();
    }
    else {                       // neither — nothing can apply
        error(7);
    }
}
The principle hiding in that code

The parser chose its rule by looking at one single token. For that to be possible, no two alternatives of the same non-terminal may start with the same token.

A grammar satisfying this is called LL(1) [scan Left to right, build a Leftmost derivation, using 1 token of lookahead]. When two alternatives do start alike, you repair it with left factoring — chapter 19.

Left recursion: the infinite loop

Everything in chapter 12 works — until you feed it the grammar from chapter 6. Then the program hangs. This is the single most important bug in top-down parsing.

E → E + T | T            ← the grammar

func E() {               ← the parser you are FORCED to write
    E();                 ← calls itself IMMEDIATELY
    if (token != '+') error();
    else token = nextToken();
    T();
}
E() called
  → E() called      (no token consumed)
    → E() called    (no token consumed)
      → E() called  ...
                    STACK OVERFLOW
Definition — left recursion

A rule is left-recursive when the non-terminal on the left reappears as the very first symbol on the right.

A → A α         ← left-recursive   ✗
A → α A         ← right-recursive  ✓  (harmless)
Trace it and watch it die
  1. E() is called. Its first action is to call E().
  2. That E() is in exactly the same situation — no token was consumed. Its first action is to call E().
  3. …forever, until the call stack overflows.

The parser never even glances at the input. No base case, because nothing ever makes progress.

The sentence to write in an examLeft recursion makes a recursive descent parser loop infinitely, because the recursive call occurs before any input is consumed, so the parser never makes progress and the recursion never terminates.

Only the first position matters. If anything gets consumed before the recursive call, the parser makes progress and terminates.

And you cannot simply avoid writing it

Chapter 7: left-associative operators force left recursion. Your notes say exactly this — "left recursion occurs due to left associative operators". Since − and / must be left-associative, the natural grammar is always left-recursive.

So we cannot avoid writing it. We must transform it.

     no  → finished
One honest caveat

Longest-prefix does not always finish in one round. For S → a S S b S | a S a S b | a b b | b you still need two rounds either way, because after factoring out a S from the first two, the result and a b b still share an a. "Longest" reduces the number of rounds; it does not guarantee one.

The dangling else

REAL CODE — this is the whole problem

if (a)
    if (b)
        x = 1;
    else                  // ← which if does this else belong to?
        x = 2;
Reading 1 — inner if
if (a) {
    if (b)  x = 1;
    else    x = 2;
}

a = true,  b = false
                → x = 2
a = false
                → nothing
Reading 2 — outer if
if (a) {
    if (b)  x = 1;
}
else        x = 2;

a = true,  b = false
                → nothing
a = false
                → x = 2
SAME TEXT.  TWO MEANINGS.

a = false  →  reading 1 does nothing
              reading 2 sets x = 2

The grammar from your notes

S → i E t S | i E t S e S | a
E → b

i = if      E = condition      t = then
e = else    a = any other statement
the parser's problem:

   token = i   →  which rule?

   S → i E t S        ?
   S → i E t S e S    ?

both start with  i E t S
the 'e' that tells them apart is MILES away
one lookahead token is useless here

Factor it

common prefix:  i E t S       ← the longest shared part

α  = i E t S
β₁ = ε          (the first rule ends exactly at the prefix)
β₂ = e S

S  → i E t S S′ | a
S′ → e S | ε
E  → b
now the parser works:

   read  i E t S         // no decision needed
   reach S′
   if (token == 'e')  →  there IS an else
   else               →  ε, no else
What this fixes — and what it does not

Fixed: the parser can now proceed. It reads i E t S, arrives at S′, and asks one question — is the next token e?

Not fixed: the ambiguity is still there. In the nested case, when S′ sees an e, the inner S′ and the outer S′ are both waiting and both would accept it.

So you are facing two problems at once

PROBLEM 1 — NON-DETERMINISM        "I can't pick a rule"

   S → i E t S          both start with  i E t S
   S → i E t S e S      parser is stuck at the very first 'i'

PROBLEM 2 — AMBIGUITY              "I picked rules, but two ways work"

   tree A: else under INNER if
   tree B: else under OUTER if
   both are legal derivations of the same string
AFTER LEFT FACTORING

   problem 1:  FIXED       ✓
   problem 2:  STILL THERE ✗

The fix for problem 2

A rule bolted on from outside the grammar 
an ELSE binds to the NEAREST UNMATCHED IF

So Reading 1 wins. Every real language does this. In practice the parser is simply told: when S′ sees e, take it — and the inner S′ grabs it because it asks first.

That is a decision, not a derivation. The grammar stays ambiguous forever; the parser is given a tie-breaker. (Chapter 9's precedence declarations are the same idea.)

The distinction worth keepingLeft factoring cures non-determinism (can the parser choose?). It does not cure ambiguity (does the string have one meaning?). Different diseases, different cures.

Chapter 22

Cheat sheet

The story, start to finish

  1. Regular expressions describe tokens — but cannot describe nesting, because a finite machine has no notepad and cannot count without a bound.
  2. Grammars describe structure. G = (N, T, S, P). Recursion in the rules gives nesting to any depth.
  3. A derivation rewrites from the start symbol to terminals. A parse tree shows the same thing as structure, and the tree is what the compiler uses.
  4. Ambiguity = two parse trees for one string = two meanings. Fatal: no top-down parser can be written.
  5. Precedence is fixed by layering: one non-terminal per level, loosest at the top, atoms at the bottom.
  6. Associativity is fixed by which side the recursion sits on. Left recursion → left-associative; right recursion → right-associative.
  7. Recursive descent: one function per non-terminal, one if-check per terminal, an error index at every failure point.
  8. Left recursion makes that parser loop forever. Remove it, then restore left-grouping with a loop.
  9. Non-determinism stops the parser choosing. Left-factor it.

The two recipes

LEFT RECURSION REMOVAL
    A → A α₁ | A α₂ | β₁ | β₂
    ⇒   A  → β₁ A′ | β₂ A′
        A′ → α₁ A′ | α₂ A′ | ε

LEFT FACTORING
    A → α β₁ | α β₂ | γ
    ⇒   A  → α A′ | γ
        A′ → β₁ | β₂

Indirect left recursion — the procedure

  1. Number the non-terminals A₁ … Aₙ and keep that order.
  2. For each Aᵢ, substitute every earlier Aⱼ (j < i) appearing at the front of one of its rules.
  3. The left recursion is now visible. Remove it with the direct recipe.
  4. Move to Aᵢ₊₁.

Which error branch?

Non-terminalMismatch branch
has an ε alternativereturn — quietly
no ε alternativeerror(n)

Three things that get confused in exams

ProblemSymptomCure
Ambiguityone string, two parse treeslayering — by hand, no algorithm exists
Left recursionparser loops foreverA → β A′, A′ → α A′ | ε
Non-determinismparser cannot choose a ruleleft factoring

Order of operations

1. remove ambiguity      (layering)
2. remove left recursion
3. left factor
4. NOW write the recursive descent parser

In that order. Removing left recursion can create new common prefixes, so factoring comes after — not before.

The grammar to reproduce from memory

E → E + T | E − T | T          + and −  : loosest, LEFT-associative
T → T * P | T / P | P          * and /  : tighter, LEFT-associative
P → F ^ P | F                  ^        : tightest op, RIGHT-associative
F → int   | ( E )              atoms, and the bracket escape hatch

From this alone you should be able to: draw the tree for any expression; show it is unambiguous; remove its left recursion; write the recursive descent functions.

What comes next in the course

  • FIRST and FOLLOW sets — the formal test for whether one lookahead token really is enough.
  • LL(1) parsing tables — recursive descent turned into a table plus a loop, exactly as the scanner was in Part 1, chapter 20. These transformations are its entry requirements.
  • LR parsing (SLR, LALR) — bottom-up; handles left recursion natively, so none of this surgery is needed there. This is what yacc and bison generate.
